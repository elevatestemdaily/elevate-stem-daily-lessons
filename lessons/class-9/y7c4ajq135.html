Today's Science Mystery

How do scientists know the universe is expanding?

10 min readDaily STEMClass 9
Today's Mission

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain what redshift is, describe Hubble's discovery, and predict how the light from a galaxy moving away at higher speed would appear.

1. The mystery

In 1929, Edwin Hubble made one of the greatest discoveries in science: almost every galaxy is moving away from us. The farther away it is, the faster it's moving. What could this mean about the history — and future — of the universe?

2. The science behind it

Redshift: When a light source moves away from an observer, the wavelength of emitted light is stretched — shifted toward longer (redder) wavelengths. This is the Doppler effect for light. - Blueshift: source approaching — wavelength compressed → shifted toward blue - Redshift: source receding → shifted toward red

Measuring redshift: Atoms emit light at specific wavelengths (spectral lines — e.g., hydrogen emits at 656.3 nm in red). If a galaxy moves away, these lines shift to longer wavelengths. The shift tells us velocity: z = Δλ/λ = v/c (for v << c).

Hubble's discovery (1929): Hubble measured distances to galaxies (using Cepheid variable stars as "standard candles") and their recession velocities (from redshift). He found: v = H₀ × d (Hubble's Law). H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc (per megaparsec of distance). Every additional megaparsec of distance adds ~70 km/s of recession speed.

Implication: Run the expansion backward → all galaxies converge to a single point ~13.8 billion years ago → the Big Bang.

Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB): The afterglow of the Big Bang — uniformly detected in all directions as microwave radiation at ~2.7 K. Confirmed by Penzias & Wilson (1965, Nobel 1978). The CMB is the most redshifted radiation known — originally X-rays, now stretched to microwaves by 13.8 billion years of expansion.

3. Build the mental model

Take a rubber balloon and draw three dots on it with a marker — label them A, B, and C. Now inflate the balloon slowly. Watch what happens: every dot moves away from every other dot. Dot A sees B and C receding. Dot C sees A and B receding. No dot is "at the centre" — every dot thinks it is the centre of expansion.

Now notice something crucial: dots that started farther apart move apart faster during inflation. A dot 4 cm from you recedes twice as fast as a dot 2 cm from you. This is exactly Hubble's Law — recession velocity is proportional to distance — and it requires no centre point.

The rubber surface is two-dimensional; the universe is three-dimensional. But the logic is identical. Space itself is stretching, carrying galaxies apart like dots on an inflating balloon. The stretching also stretches light waves travelling through space: each wavelength gets pulled longer — redshifted — in proportion to how much space expanded during the light's journey. A galaxy 10 billion light-years away has its light stretched because space expanded enormously while that light was travelling toward us.

5. Real-world connection

India's Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT, Pune) has contributed to measurements of hydrogen (21 cm line) redshift in distant galaxies, mapping the large-scale structure of the universe. AstroSat (ISRO, 2015) observes UV and X-ray from distant galaxies whose temperatures and distances are derived from their spectral shifts.

The Square Kilometre Array (SKA), of which India is a founding partner, will when complete be the world's largest radio telescope — capable of detecting redshifted hydrogen from the early universe (z > 6, when the universe was less than 1 billion years old). Indian radio astronomers at TIFR and NCRA-Pune have discovered new radio galaxies and active galactic nuclei using redshift measurements. The same Doppler physics a Class 9 student learns in a lesson on sound pitch changes is the mathematical foundation of modern observational cosmology.

6. Mini activity — 5 minutes

Rubber band universe: Cut an elastic band open to form a long strip. Mark five dots along it at regular 5 cm intervals — label them G1 through G5. You are standing at G3. Measure the distance from G3 to each other galaxy: G2 is 5 cm, G1 is 10 cm; G4 is 5 cm, G5 is 10 cm.

Now stretch the band to twice its length (hold it against a ruler). Measure again: G2 is now 10 cm (moved 5 cm), G1 is now 20 cm (moved 10 cm). G1 moved twice as far in the same stretch — so from G3's perspective, G1 receded twice as fast as G2. This is Hubble's Law in a physical model: every dot sees more distant dots receding faster. Ask a partner to observe from G1 — they will see the same pattern. No galaxy is special; no galaxy is at the centre.

7. Think deeper

Dark energy: Since 1998, astronomers have found that the universe's expansion is accelerating. Distant Type Ia supernovae (standard candles — all have the same intrinsic brightness) appear dimmer than expected — they are farther than Hubble's Law predicts. Some energy is pushing space apart — dubbed "dark energy." It constitutes ~68% of the universe's total energy content. Dark matter: ~27%. Ordinary matter (you, stars, planets): ~5%. Despite the precision of this accounting, 95% of the universe is of unknown composition.

The 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess for discovering accelerating expansion. The biggest open question in physics today: what is dark energy? If its density increases as space expands, the universe may end in a "Big Rip" — space expanding so fast that even atoms are torn apart. If it declines, expansion may slow and reverse into a "Big Crunch."

Predict: If dark energy causes the expansion rate to double over the next 10 billion years, what would happen to the redshift of a galaxy currently at z = 1? Would future astronomers see more galaxies or fewer compared to what we see today — and why?

8. Quick quiz

Q1

What key observation provides evidence that the universe is expanding?

  • A. Scientists have measured that the total number of stars increases each year, proving the universe grows
  • B. Distant galaxies show redshift, meaning their light is stretched to longer wavelengths as they move away
  • C. Telescopes detect that all stars are moving toward Earth, indicating the universe is concentrating inward

Correct answer: B

Q2

Why is understanding the redshift mechanism more useful than just accepting that the universe expands?

  • A. Accepting expansion is sufficient; the mechanism is too mathematical for Class 9 students
  • B. A mechanism explains cause-and-effect and allows prediction in new situations
  • C. Redshift is measured by satellites so students cannot verify or understand it independently

Correct answer: B

Q3

If a galaxy were moving away from us faster than another galaxy, what would you predict about its light?

  • A. Its light would show a greater redshift with wavelengths stretched more toward the red end of the spectrum
  • B. Its light would appear bluer because faster motion compresses the wavelengths toward shorter values
  • C. Its light would show no shift because speed only affects the brightness of distant objects

Correct answer: A

Challenge

A spectroscopist observes a distant galaxy and detects its hydrogen emission line at 820 nm instead of the standard 656.3 nm.

Calculate the redshift z = (λ_observed − λ_rest) / λ_rest. Using the approximation v = z × c (where c = 3 × 10⁵ km/s), estimate the recession velocity of the galaxy. Then use Hubble's Law (H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc) to estimate the galaxy's distance in megaparsecs. If the expansion rate were to increase by 20%, would you expect the redshift of this galaxy to appear higher or lower to a future astronomer measuring the same galaxy? Justify your answer in terms of what happens to space and wavelengths.

10. One surprising idea

The Andromeda Galaxy — our nearest large galactic neighbour — shows a blueshift, not a redshift. It is moving toward us at roughly 110 km/s and will collide with the Milky Way in approximately 4.5 billion years. This seems to contradict Hubble's Law, but it does not: at close distances, galaxies have their own gravitational peculiar velocities that can overpower the expansion of space. Hubble's Law only holds reliably at cosmological distances (hundreds of megaparsecs and beyond), where the expansion of space dominates. The future collision of Andromeda and the Milky Way will produce a single giant elliptical galaxy — but stars are so far apart that almost no individual stars will physically collide. It is a collision of two mostly-empty structures of gravitationally bound points.

Daily Takeaway

The universe is expanding because space itself is stretching — galaxies carry away from each other like dots on an inflating balloon, and the stretching of space also stretches the wavelengths of light, producing the redshift that Hubble measured in 1929.
